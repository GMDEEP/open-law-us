# The Florida Bar v. Clayton Bowers Studstill

Supreme Court of Florida
                            ____________

                         No. SC2024-0149
                           ____________

                       THE FLORIDA BAR,
                         Complainant,

                                 vs.

                 CLAYTON BOWERS STUDSTILL,
                         Respondent.

                         October 15, 2026

PER CURIAM.

     The Florida Bar seeks review of a report of referee

recommending that Respondent, Clayton Bowers Studstill, be found

not guilty of professional misconduct. 1 As discussed below, we

disapprove the referee’s findings of fact and recommendation that

Respondent be found not guilty of violating any Rules Regulating

The Florida Bar. We instead find Respondent guilty of violating

rules 4-3.3 (Candor Toward the Tribunal) and 4-3.4 (Fairness to

Opposing Party and Counsel), and we remand to a newly appointed

     1. We have jurisdiction. See art. V, § 15, Fla. Const.

referee for a hearing limited to a determination of the recommended

discipline.

                           BACKGROUND

     On January 31, 2024, the Bar filed a formal complaint against

Respondent, alleging that while appearing before the Ninth Judicial

Circuit in Orange County case number 2017-DR-012388, he

violated rules 4-3.3 and 4-3.4. It alleged he knowingly failed to

abide by the court-ordered rule of sequestration by speaking to his

expert witness and then, when questioned about his misconduct,

misrepresented to the court what he had said to the witness.

Respondent and Dorothy McMichen were together representing

Respondent’s wife in a paternity proceeding brought by her former

boyfriend, Timothy Joseph Galvin. The Court referred the matter to

a referee for consideration.

        Referee’s Findings as to Alleged Rule Violations

     The Galvin paternity case was scheduled for trial the week of

June 6, 2022. On Friday, June 3, 2022, Respondent and

McMichen met in Orlando to finalize preparations for trial. The two

lawyers had divided the witnesses between them. After having

dinner together and returning to McMichen’s office, McMichen

                                -2-

noticed that Respondent looked “red-faced” and “acted different.”

Respondent left McMichen’s office early that evening and agreed to

return the following morning. However, Respondent did not meet

with McMichen the following day but instead went to Guidewell

Emergency Doctors in Ocoee, Florida, where he tested positive for

COVID.

     On June 5, 2022, McMichen filed a motion to continue in the

Galvin case based on Respondent’s illness. On the morning of June

6, 2022, a virtual hearing on the motion was held before Judge

Michael Kraynick. After Mrs. Studstill testified pertaining to

Respondent’s condition, Judge Kraynick questioned Respondent

regarding his illness and then granted in part and denied in part

the motion to continue. The trial was continued from June 6 to

June 8, and Mrs. Studstill and both of her attorneys were allowed

to appear virtually.

     On June 8, 2022, Respondent appeared virtually for trial. He

conducted the direct examination of one witness. Respondent told

Judge Kraynick that he was feeling better that day. At the

disciplinary hearing, McMichen, the guardian ad litem Mary Ann

Etzler, opposing counsel Douglas Dorner, and Timothy Galvin all

                                -3-

testified that Respondent had no apparent issues and that there

were no signs he was unwell. Respondent testified that on the

evening of June 8, 2022, he took an at-home COVID test and again

tested positive.

     Respondent believed on Thursday, June 9, 2022, he had no

choice but to appear in person for trial, as his client’s key witness,

Dr. Lisa Bidwell, was testifying that day and Respondent had

approximately 10,000 emails to get into evidence. Despite not being

fully recovered, even at one point asking Dr. Bidwell if she felt the

building was swaying, he was able to direct examine Dr. Bidwell,

enter some exhibits into evidence, and respond to objections.

     During Dr. Bidwell’s testimony, an issue arose regarding the

authentication of text messages. Before her testimony was

complete, Dr. Bidwell was asked to wait in the hallway until the

documents could be authenticated by Mrs. Studstill. Prior to her

departure, the judge invoked the rule of sequestration, explained

what it meant, and asked Dr. Bidwell if she understood. She

acknowledged that she understood. The trial judge expressly

instructed Dr. Bidwell that she was “not permitted to have any

contact or communication with any party or any party’s

                                 -4-

representative between now and the time that [she was] called again

to testify.” Respondent remained in the courtroom while McMichen

questioned Mrs. Studstill. As the examination was wrapping up,

Respondent grabbed a stack of papers and exited the courtroom.

After the judge adjourned for lunch, opposing counsel Dorner

notified the bailiff that he needed to speak with the judge. Dorner

advised the judge that he had observed Respondent outside the

courtroom sitting and speaking to Dr. Bidwell. Judge Kraynick

called Respondent back into the courtroom and questioned him

about his conduct.

     Respondent told the judge he was releasing Dr. Bidwell as a

witness and wishing her well. He further stated: “I thought that she

would be coming back to testify after the communications had been

authenticated. Absent the authentication, I was trying to turn off

the meter and release the witness.” In response, Judge Kraynick

asked: “How do I know that you were not out there talking to her

about what exhibits were entered and what were not, and now

you’re telling me you’re releasing her without Mr. Dorner even

having the opportunity to cross[-]examine her? You could

understand the [c]ourt’s frustration, right?” Respondent then

                                -5-

changed his story, stating “[t]hat was my goal in going outside to

make sure she was here.”

      Judge Kraynick then called Dr. Bidwell back into the

courtroom and questioned her about her conversation with

Respondent. She said, “He was talking about the text messages

that weren’t admitted for my -- in my analysis [and] was referencing

the exhibit numbers that he thought weren’t coming in.” When

questioned further, she informed the court that Respondent had not

had any conversation with her about releasing her as a witness.

      Judge Kraynick then asked Respondent, “Why would you lie to

this tribunal?” In response, Respondent stated that he did not

believe he lied to the tribunal, but he agreed “that there must not

have been proper candor.” Respondent also said, “In that moment,

I did not recall properly,” and that he was paying attention “[t]o the

best of [his] ability.”

      The judge released everyone for the lunch break. Upon trial

resuming, Respondent was not present. McMichen informed the

judge that Respondent was not feeling well and had left to seek

medical attention. Judge Kraynick requested that Respondent

return to the court. Upon his return, Respondent told the judge

                                 -6-

that his wife and co-counsel told him he did not look well, he did

not feel well, and he needed to go get checked out before returning

to work. Respondent also told the judge that he did not feel like he

was “making very good decisions at that moment, and that [his]

ability to practice at the moment is hampered” and that he had

“some COVID brain.” The judge then released Respondent to seek

medical attention.

     Respondent returned to Guidewell Emergency Doctors,

complaining of “not thinking right.” Respondent was admitted to

Orlando Health Hospital the afternoon of June 9, 2022, and was

discharged from the hospital the following day. The discharge

summary states that Respondent’s diagnosis was COVID-19 and

that he “[r]eturned to work too soon after Covid-19 while still

symptomatic.”

     Dr. Khurram Nazir, a board-certified neurologist, testified as

an expert witness at the disciplinary hearing on Respondent’s

behalf. Dr. Nazir reviewed all of Respondent’s Orlando medical

records and met with Respondent in July 2024. In Dr. Nazir’s

opinion, Respondent was suffering from delirium on June 9, 2022,

and should not have been working. Dr. Nazir testified that a person

                                 -7-

suffering from delirium may appear “normal” one moment and have

loss of executive function the next.

             Referee’s Recommendations as to Guilt

     In recommending that Respondent be found not guilty of the

charged rule violations, the referee first stated that

          [t]here’s no question that the statements made by
     Mr. Studstill and his behavior in and of themselves on
     the record on June 9, 2022, are both a violation of the
     judge’s order of sequestration, which is very clear on the
     record, and is a violation of the disciplinary rule of not
     being honest and candid with the court.
          On their face the statements are clearly that.
     Normally, that would be the end of the discussion. We’d
     be moving to a sanctions hearing, and that’d be that.

     The referee believed, however, that the case “should never

have happened” and that “[t]here are a whole lot of oddities.” The

referee discussed that COVID was “a very divisive kind of thing” and

that he never had a situation where a judge did not take a lawyer’s

word that he was ill or sick.

     Regarding Respondent’s conduct with respect to Dr. Bidwell,

the referee made the following observation:

          They’re in the middle of the testimony of Mrs.
     Studstill to authenticate these emails, and Mr. Studstill
     walks outside and has a conversation in violation of the
     sequestration order with Dr. Bidwell. That makes no
     sense to me at all for a person in their right mind.

                                  -8-

     Especially a lawyer.
           Why am I, as a lawyer, going to walk outside the
     courtroom and start talking to a witness out there?
     Number one, it’s a violation of the judge’s order, but
     that’s beside the point. And start talking to them about
     what is or isn’t coming into evidence when the judge
     hasn’t even made a ruling? That doesn’t make any sense
     whatsoever. It is absolutely nonsensical. It just wouldn’t
     even begin to make sense.

     The referee then discussed what he believed was a nonsensical

answer by Respondent in response to the trial court’s inquiry:

           Mr. Studstill compounds the problem by, when
     being addressed by the judge, well, what did you say to
     her? Well, I told her to go home. Why would he tell her
     to do that either? She’s not been excused, but that’s
     beside the point. By the witness going home, it would
     torpedo his own case. It would make no sense for him to
     do that either. It makes no logical sense.
           And I’m putting all this in this report to hopefully
     make it real clear to the Supreme Court Justices when
     they look at this, that this just doesn’t make any sense,
     and why it doesn’t make any sense, and it just basically
     doesn’t make any sense in this situation.

     The referee could not find that Respondent acted knowingly

because

     [t]he statements he made that were both inappropriate
     and untruthful make no sense for him to have made in
     his normal right mind.
           They don’t logically help his case. Most people
     when they lie, lie to help themselves or lie to help their
     client, unfortunately. That’s what they do. This makes
     no sense because none of this was going to do anything
     to help Mr. Studstill at all. It never was going to make

                                -9-

        any sense.

        Based upon his impressions that Respondent’s conduct did

not make sense, the referee recommended that the Bar’s complaint

be dismissed and Respondent be found not guilty of violating any

provision of the rules. The referee further stated that had the

underlying case simply been continued by a month, this case would

not be before the Court. In addition, the referee added that nothing

he heard about Respondent suggests “this was in any way, shape,

or form consistent with his behavior in the 20 years he’s practiced

law.”

                                 ANALYSIS

        “The Court’s review of the referee’s findings of fact is limited,

and if a referee’s findings of fact are supported by competent,

substantial evidence in the record, this Court will not reweigh the

evidence and substitute its judgment for that of the referee.” Fla.

Bar v. Schwartz, 284 So. 3d 393, 396 (Fla. 2019) (citing Fla. Bar v.

Frederick, 756 So. 2d 79, 86 (Fla. 2000)). “That is, ‘a referee’s

findings of fact regarding guilt carry a presumption of correctness

that should be upheld unless clearly erroneous or without support

in the record.’ ” Id. (quoting Fla. Bar v. Barrett, 897 So. 2d 1269,

                                    - 10 -

1275 (Fla. 2005)). Further, the party challenging the referee’s

findings of fact and recommendations as to guilt has the burden to

demonstrate “that there is no evidence in the record to support

those findings or that the record evidence clearly contradicts the

conclusions.” Fla. Bar v. Germain, 957 So. 2d 613, 620 (Fla. 2007).

However, as to the actual recommendations of guilt, “the referee’s

factual findings must be sufficient under the applicable rules to

support the recommendations.” Fla. Bar v. Patterson, 257 So. 3d

56, 61 (Fla. 2018) (citing Fla. Bar v. Shoureas, 913 So. 2d 554, 557-

58 (Fla. 2005)).

     Rule 4-3.3(a)(1) provides that “[a] lawyer shall not

knowingly . . . make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal or

fail to correct a false statement of material fact or law previously

made to the tribunal by the lawyer.” Rule 4-3.4(c) states “[a] lawyer

must not knowingly disobey an obligation under the rules of a

tribunal except for an open refusal based on an assertion that no

valid obligation exists.” Both rules require that the lawyer act

“knowingly.” The record contains no evidence to support the

referee’s findings. Indeed, the evidence contradicts the referee’s

conclusions that Respondent did not act knowingly.

                                 - 11 -

     To support the finding that Respondent’s misconduct was not

done knowingly, the referee erroneously focused on what

Respondent’s motive would be, concluding that because the

misconduct would make no sense, he could not have acted

knowingly. The question is not, however, Respondent’s motive, but

whether he acted knowingly, i.e., deliberately, Black’s Law

Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), with awareness, deliberateness, or

intention, Webster’s Third New International Dictionary (1993 ed.).

See Fla. Bar v. Watson, 76 So. 3d 915, 922 (Fla. 2011). The record

clearly establishes that Respondent’s conduct was not accidental

but deliberate.

     First, the referee made clearly erroneous findings about when

Judge Kraynick ruled on the admissibility of the exhibits and when

Respondent left the courtroom to go speak to Dr. Bidwell.

     At the hearing before the referee, the guardian ad litem

testified that Respondent remained in the courtroom at counsel

table on June 9, 2022, for about 10-15 minutes before the lunch

break before taking papers and a folder with him when he left.

The trial transcript reflects that a break was taken at 10:53 a.m.,

and the court reconvened at 11:00 a.m. for the authentication

                                - 12 -

testimony. The lunch break was taken at 12:16 p.m., so, because

Respondent only left the courtroom 10-15 minutes before the lunch

break, he sat through approximately one hour of testimony before

leaving the courtroom. Thus, while the trial transcript does not

reflect at exactly what point Respondent went to speak to Dr.

Bidwell, it is clear from the record Respondent left after Judge

Kraynick had made at least some ruling or rulings on admissibility.

Indeed, Judge Kraynick observed Respondent leave the courtroom

and asked when the issue was raised, “How do I know that you

were not out there talking to her about what exhibits were entered

and what were not?”

     When questioned by the court, Respondent did not admit that

he was discussing the exclusion of certain exhibits with his expert

witness, which would have risked having his witness’s testimony

entirely stricken. Rather, he falsely told the judge he was releasing

her. Respondent’s story fell apart when Judge Kraynick questioned

the expert witness, who denied that Respondent had discharged

her. She specifically testified that he discussed what exhibits would

not be admitted. As is the case here, “absent an admission by the

charged person, intent to violate a court order may be established

                                - 13 -

by circumstantial evidence.” Fla. Bar v. Forrester, 916 So. 2d 647,

652 (Fla. 2005).

     Second, the referee failed to view Respondent’s alleged

misconduct in the context of his actions throughout the paternity

proceedings, stating that “nobody has ever presented anything

about Mr. Studstill that suggested this was in any way, shape, or

form consistent with his behavior in the 15 years or more that he

has practiced law.” Contrary to this finding of fact, Respondent had

previously been found to have been in violation of one of the parties’

joint stipulations by Judge Kraynick. And it is proper to consider

the trial court’s final order on paternity that was entered into

evidence in the disciplinary hearing. Fla. Bar v. Gwynn, 94 So. 3d

425, 430 (Fla. 2012) (“The Court has clearly held that a referee in a

bar disciplinary proceeding can properly rely upon facts established

in orders and decisions of other tribunals to support his or her

findings of fact.”); Fla. Bar v. Alva, 404 So. 3d 293, 298 (Fla. 2024).

     Finally, regarding Respondent’s medical condition and its

effect on his conduct, the referee’s findings are also clearly

erroneous. While there is no dispute that Respondent tested

positive for COVID at a medical facility on June 4, 2022, the

                                 - 14 -

witness testimony was conflicting as to Respondent’s physical

symptoms. None of the medical records from June 9, 2022, reflect

that Respondent was running a fever when he was seen at either

urgent care or the emergency room, or that he was not oriented and

alert. Rather, the medical records only include Respondent’s self-

reports of “not thinking right” and possibly “experiencing brain fog.”

Moreover, as contemporaneously found by Judge Kraynick only

hours before, Respondent had made his entry of appearance,

addressed the court regarding the nature of Dr. Bidwell’s testimony,

began the direct examination of Dr. Bidwell and then requested a

break in order to recall the witness after laying a foundation. In

addition, for the first time when testifying before the referee,

Respondent claimed that he tested positive for COVID the evening

of June 8, 2022, but did not advise the trial court of that fact. Nor

did McMichen testify before the referee that Respondent mentioned

anything about a positive COVID test on June 8. Further, while

Respondent testified before the referee that he tested positive for

COVID at the hospital on June 9, 2022, the records refute that

testimony by not reflecting any new diagnosis of COVID-19, only

that he had previously been diagnosed with the virus on June 4.

                                 - 15 -

And regarding Dr. Nazir’s testimony, once again, it is based upon

Respondent’s self-reports, both from the 2022 records and his 2024

evaluation of Respondent. For these reasons, we conclude that the

medical records and testimony here do not constitute competent,

substantial evidence that Respondent was not “in his normal right

mind” and thus did not act knowingly as found by the referee. Cf.

Fla. Bar v. Whitney, 132 So. 3d 1095, 1104 (Fla. 2013) (competent,

substantial evidence supported the referee’s finding that the

representation to the tribunal was false regarding why the

respondent could not attend the deposition because respondent’s

medical records only showed that he went to a medical clinic two

days after the deposition he failed to attend, was treated for a skin

rash, and at no time filed a notice of unavailability for the

deposition or contacted opposing counsel).

     Based upon the foregoing, the referee’s findings of fact are

clearly erroneous. As discussed above, the record evidence

establishes that Respondent knowingly violated the rule of

sequestration, as ordered by the court, and discussed with his

expert witness some of the exhibits that would be excluded from

trial, in violation of rule 4-3.4(c). The record evidence also

                                 - 16 -

establishes that Respondent then knowingly lied to Judge Kraynick

regarding his interaction with his expert witness in violation of rule

4-3.3(a)(1).

     The dissent disagrees, arguing that Respondent did not violate

the “rule of sequestration.” While the dissent may be correct about

the traditional scope of sequestration, that is beside the point. The

trial court did not simply invoke the rule of sequestration in

generalized terms. Instead, Respondent disregarded the trial

court’s specific sequestration ruling that Dr. Bidwell not speak to

“any party or any party’s representative” until she was recalled to

testify. That is, he “knowingly disobey[ed]” the ruling without

“open[ly] refus[ing]”—or objecting to—the obligation it imposed. R.

Regulating Fla. Bar 4-3.4(c). In doing so, he also placed the witness

in an untenable situation. If Respondent felt the trial judge’s

sequestration order was overbroad or incorrect, he should have

raised his concerns with the judge in the presence of opposing

counsel—not ignore the order of the court altogether.

                            CONCLUSION

     Accordingly, we conclude that the referee’s conclusion that

Respondent did not violate Rules Regulating The Florida Bar

                                - 17 -

4-3.3(a)(1) and 4-3.4(c) is clearly erroneous. Therefore, we

disapprove the referee’s report and remand this case to a newly

appointed referee for a hearing limited to a determination of

recommended discipline.

     It is so ordered.

TANENBAUM, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part.

     Alas, the majority finds the respondent guilty of “disobey[ing]

an obligation under the rules of a tribunal,” Rule Regulating the

Florida Bar 4-3.4(c), even though the old, common-law

sequestration rule codified in evidentiary rule 90.616 did not and

does not preclude him from doing what he did—talk to an expert

witness during a break in testimony. At the core of the

respondent’s wrongdoing is his lack of candor to the trial court, and

I concur that he thereby violated Rule Regulating the Florida Bar 4-

3.3. Otherwise, I dissent.

                                - 18 -

     The bar initiated disciplinary proceedings by alleging that, in

addition to being untruthful to the trial judge, the respondent

“fail[ed] to abide by the rules of sequestration set down by the court

for the proceedings” when he talked to his own expert witness

during a break. The rule of sequestration, though, is not as broad

as the bar makes it out to be.

     The rule allows the trial court to exclude a witness “from a

proceeding so that they cannot hear the testimony of other

witnesses.” Fla. R. Evid. § 90.616(1) (emphasis supplied). As the

highlighted text indicates, “[t]he purpose of the rule of sequestration

is ‘to avoid a witness coloring his or her testimony by hearing the

testimony of another,’ thereby discouraging ‘fabrication, inaccuracy

and collusion.’” Knight v. State, 746 So. 2d 423, 430 (Fla. 1998)

(quoting CHARLES W. EHRHARDT, FLORIDA EVIDENCE § 616.1, at 506

(1998 ed.)); see also Hilton v. State, 117 So. 3d 742, 751 (Fla. 2013)

(noting that the “reason for the rule of witness sequestration is to

avoid coloring a witness’s testimony by that heard from other

witnesses”).

     Strictly speaking, when the rule is invoked, a witness is not “to

remain in the courtroom during proceedings when he or she is not

                                 - 19 -

on the witness stand.” Burns v. State, 609 So. 2d 600, 606 (Fla.

1992). That is all the rule requires. Cf. Fed. R. Evid. 615(b) (stating

that an order under the preceding subdivision, comparable to

Florida’s evidentiary rule 90.616(1), “operates only to exclude

witnesses from the courtroom”). As any experienced trial lawyer

knows, the rule itself does not offer a blanket preclusion against

discussions between an attorney and his witness about testimony

the witness will give. See Kingery v. State, 523 So. 2d 1199, 1204

(Fla. 1st DCA 1988) (“It is undisputed that an attorney may talk to a

witness about the testimony the witness will give, and that the

witness’s credibility should not be challenged on the basis of the

discussion.”); see also Nieves v. State, 739 So. 2d 125, 127 (Fla. 5th

DCA 1999) (“We do not think the rule of sequestration prohibits an

attorney from talking with a witness about the testimony he or she

will give in a future court appearance.”).

     The standard civil and criminal jury instructions posted to the

bar’s own website acknowledge this basic point. See Fla. Std. Jury

Instr. (Civ.) 601.2(c) (“[It is entirely proper for a lawyer to talk to a

witness about what testimony the witness would give if called to the

courtroom. The witness should not be discredited just because the

                                   - 20 -

witness talked with a lawyer about [his] [her] testimony.]”); Fla. Std.

Jury Instr. (Crim.) 3.9 (same); Florida Standard Jury Instructions,

The Florida Bar, https://www.floridabar.org/rules/florida-

standard-jury-instructions/.

     Moreover, experts typically offer opinion testimony based on

evidence presented through other witnesses. For this reason,

experts routinely are excepted from the sequestration rule. Cf. Fla.

R. Evid. § 90.616(2)(c) (providing for exception from exclusion rule

for a witness “whose presence is shown by the party’s attorney to be

essential to the presentation of the party’s cause”); Knight, 746

So. 2d at 430 (explaining that “[t]his exception is applied most

commonly to expert witnesses because ‘experts are testifying to

their opinions rather than to factual matters.’” (quoting EHRHARDT,

FLORIDA EVIDENCE, § 616.1, at 510)). There is no indication that

opposing counsel requested that the expert or the respondent be

specially ordered not to communicate at all about anything. So

the trial judge’s statement in open court that the expert witness was

“not permitted to have any contact or communication with any

party or any party’s representative” could only be understood as an

attempt quickly to summarize for the expert witness, albeit

                                - 21 -

inaccurately, what the rule of sequestration meant in layman’s

terms.

     The majority says this is all “beside the point.” Yet the rule of

sequestration, on this record, is the whole point. Contrary to the

majority’s characterization, there was no “sequestration ruling.”

Opposing counsel simply and routinely “invoked” the “rule” at the

start of the evidentiary proceeding (yes, that rule, which the

majority says does not matter), and the trial judge noted it would

apply to all witnesses, with one exception not relevant here. That

was it. There was no motion asking for special conditions to be

added to the general rule of sequestration. Counsel then

“reinvoked” the rule when the parties broke for lunch—again,

without any request for a special variation on the rule. At no point,

then, was there a “ruling” by the trial judge. The “rule”—that is,

rule 90.616—was invoked, and the rule was in effect.

     There being no special “ruling,” it cannot fairly be said that the

judge directly ordered the respondent to do or not do anything. The

rule itself was in effect, and the respondent was subject to the rule’s

general terms of preclusion. The trial judge’s effort to explain to the

expert what invocation of the rule meant in terms that did not fully

                                - 22 -

comport with the rule is not the same as “ordering” other terms as a

ruling on a motion.

     The respondent, in turn, should not be ethically faulted for not

realizing the trial judge was expanding the rule of sequestration far

beyond what most practicing attorneys understand the rule to

mean. To be sure, had there been evidence that the respondent

attempted to undermine or circumvent the purpose of the rule—to

guard against a fact witness’s being exposed to in-court trial

testimony—there likely would have been an ethical breach to be

addressed here. Cf. Geders v. United States, 425 U.S. 80, 87 (1976)

(explaining the three-fold purpose behind the ancient English

sequestration practice as (1) restraining witnesses from “‘tailoring’

their testimony to that of earlier witnesses”; (2) making it easier to

“detect[] testimony that is less than candid”; and (3) “preventing

improper attempts to influence the testimony”); Fed. R. Evid. 615,

Advisory Committee Notes (2023) (explaining that rule revision

“emphasizes that the court may by order extend the sequestration

beyond the courtroom, to prohibit those subject to the order from

disclosing trial testimony to excluded witnesses” and that the rule

does not “bar[] a court from prohibiting counsel from disclosing trial

                                 - 23 -

testimony to a sequestered witness” (emphasis supplied)).

     No one suggests the respondent attempted to improperly

influence his own expert’s testimony here. The expert obviously did

not have personal knowledge about the e-mails that presumably

were going to factor into her opinion testimony—which is why she

could not authenticate them herself. Even if the trial judge’s

instruction to the expert arguably applied to the respondent, the

expert was not being called to offer fact testimony about those e-

mails. That is, there was no risk of fact-testimony tailoring.

     Yes, the respondent’s purported discussion with his expert

during the break, clarifying which exhibits related to the expert’s

opinion testimony were being authenticated and admitted into

evidence, likely was opinion-testimony tailoring. That, however, is

acceptable trial fare. I have done this as a trial practitioner, and I

am sure many other practitioners have as well. It would be news to

many that the sequestration rule’s mere invocation would foreclose

any communication between a lawyer and his expert witness,

absent a showing of a specific need for such a blanket preclusion.
