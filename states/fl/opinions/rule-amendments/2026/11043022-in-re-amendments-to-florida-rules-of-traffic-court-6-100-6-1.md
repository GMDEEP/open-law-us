TANENBAUM, J., specially concurring.

       The Florida Constitution authorizes the Legislature to

“establish by general law a civil traffic hearing officer system for the

purpose of hearing civil traffic infractions.” Art. V, § 1, Fla. Const.

The Legislature did just that. See §§ 318.30–.38, Fla. Stat.

(establishing the Civil Traffic Infraction Hearing Officer Program).

In doing so, it tasked this court with the program’s supervision and

with adopting “rules and procedures for the establishment and

                                  -2-

operation” of the program. §§ 318.30–.31, Fla. Stat.

     By definition, matters adjudicated through this hearing officer

program are “noncriminal.” § 318.13(3), Fla. Stat. (defining

“infraction”). These matters also not being common-law suits, no

jury-trial right attaches. And no constitutional “speedy trial” right

attaches in these infraction matters either. Cf. Art. I, § 16(a), Fla.

Const. (securing to the accused “in all criminal prosecutions” the

right to “a speedy and public trial by impartial jury” (emphasis

supplied)); U.S. Const. Amend. VI (same).

     The most this court could do by rule, then, is administratively

establish a time limit for getting one of these infractions to trial—a

time limit having as its sole purpose to move these matters along

and not to establish a new right. Indeed, the court adopted Florida

Rule of Traffic Procedure 6.325 in 1992 at the rules committee’s

urging, that committee stating in part the proposed “time limit . . .

ultimately will ease the backlog of pending cases and may also ease

the daily burden upon county judges and traffic magistrates.” In re

Amends. to Fla. Rules of Prac. & Proc. for Traffic Cts., 608 So. 2d

451, 452 (Fla. 1992).

                                  -3-

     To be clear, this procedural rule does not and could not give

rise to a right (or “entitlement”) on its own. There would need to be

a statutory enactment establishing a “speedy trial” right, but

presently there is none. Any dismissal under this rule, in turn,

would have to be without prejudice, unless the limitation statute

the Legislature enacted otherwise has run. See § 775.15(2)(d), Fla.

Stat. (establishing a one-year limitation period for a “noncriminal

violation”). Yet the rule’s title (“Speedy Trial”) and its use of the

word “entitled” in subdivision (a) could prove confusing on this

front. The rule should be modified in both respects to better reflect

the rule’s purely administrative nature.
